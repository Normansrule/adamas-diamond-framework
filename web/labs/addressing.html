Addressing Lab

A coupled pair of NV centers sits 10 to 30 nm apart, but the laser focus is about 300 nm wide. Both qubits see the same light and the same microwave. Can you drive one without the other?

A pulse tuned to NV A: A flips, B should not
Two tiny magnets sit so close together that the flashlight we use to read them lights up both at once. To talk to just one, we tune our radio to a pitch only that one hears. If their pitches are too close, or we shout too loudly, both of them answer.
The focus of a lens of numerical aperture NA is an Airy spot about 0.51 λ/NA wide, so NVs 25 nm apart are optically one object. Their microwave lines can still differ: NVs on different ⟨111⟩ axes see different field projections, and a field gradient G shifts one by γₑGr. A drive of Rabi frequency Ω tuned to NV A excites NV B, detuned by Δ, with peak probability Ω²/(Ω² + Δ²). Lines must also be narrower than their separation: a line is about 1/(πT₂*) wide. Faster pulses mean more crosstalk.
Lines are exact spin-1 eigenvalues of D S_z² + γₑB·S per NV axis (adamas.addressing, golden-tested). A field along [001] projects equally on all four axes, so the spectral route vanishes; along one ⟨111⟩ axis it separates that axis from the other three by tens of MHz. Every room-temperature two-NV demonstration so far used the spectral route with joint optical readout [dolde2013] [joas2025]; field gradients have addressed NVs about 100 nm apart [grinolds2011] [zhang2017addr], and cryogenic strain or zero-phonon-line tuning has addressed three NVs in one spot [bersin2019]. Not modelled: hyperfine structure (a 2.15 MHz ¹⁴N triplet), microwave field inhomogeneity, shaped pulses (which beat this worst-case crosstalk), and readout, which stays joint in every route shown here.
