# Speaker notes: "Diamond chips, from wafer to qubit"

A 12-slide, 12 to 15 minute talk ([paper/talk.pdf](../paper/talk.pdf)) for seminars, PhD interviews, and lab visits, with an A0 poster ([paper/poster.pdf](../paper/poster.pdf)) and the preprint ([paper/adamas.pdf](../paper/adamas.pdf)). All three are built by `make talk poster paper`, and every number on them comes from the package, so they always match the code. Roughly one minute per slide.

| # | Slide | What to say | Likely question and a good answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title | One line: an open framework that follows diamond from the wafer to a quantum processor, where every number is computed and tested. | "Is this experimental work?" It is a modeling and tooling framework, validated against published measurements, with experiments designed to calibrate it. |
| 2 | Why diamond at all? | The power figure of merit and heat conduction are extreme, and the NV center is a room-temperature qubit. Then the catch: deep dopants, small wafers, p-channel-only logic. | "Why not just use SiC or GaN?" They are mature and good; the question is where diamond's margin pays for its immaturity: high voltage, high temperature, and quantum sensing. |
| 3 | What ADAMAS is | The chain from materials to experiments; validation and reproducibility are part of the design, not an afterthought. | "How do you know the models are right?" Point to the validation matrix: literature, cross-tool, and analytic checks. |
| 4 | Power switches | Real lateral devices already beat silicon's one-dimensional limit by about two orders of magnitude (a lateral device against a vertical limit); the inverter table puts it in watts, where the measured device still loses to ideal silicon carbide (SiC). | "Why lateral devices?" Hydrogen-terminated hole-gas devices are the most mature diamond transistors; vertical devices need better doping. |
| 5 | Uncertainty | Diamond wins when hot; a bulk-doped switch loses to SiC at room temperature. Only 0.50 % of the drift boron is ionized at 300 K, so SiC loss ÷ diamond loss is 0.40 at a 300 K junction and 1.9 at 523 K. Mobility (baseline 2000 cm²/V·s; 3800 is an unreproduced record) and critical field dominate. | "Why should I trust your ranges?" They are from the published spread and stated in the code; the tornado shows what to measure first. "Then why diamond?" Hot operation, high voltage on low-dislocation material, and lateral hole-gas devices, which avoid the ionization problem. |
| 6 | How you would build it | Surface chemistry makes the channel; gold goes on first; one oxygen plasma isolates; heat before metal. | "Has this flow been run?" It follows published process papers step by step; the traveler lists sources, pass criteria, and failure modes. |
| 7 | A processor on diamond | A small process design kit and a placed 4-bit processor about 1 mm² in size; emulator and Verilog agree. | "Why a 4-bit CPU?" It is the smallest complete test of a logic process, like the 4004 was for silicon. |
| 8 | Two-qubit gate | The coherence-limited model gives 0.784 to 0.998 for the published pair, depending on the echo exponent, one or two gate applications, and T₁. The measured 0.67 was already normalized to charge-preselected runs, so it lies below the whole range: a known discrepancy [dolde2013]. The earlier "charge state ready only 75 % of the time" explanation is withdrawn. The 2025 record is 96.0 ± 2.5 % at 9.5 nm with charge pre-selection [joas2025]. | "How do you address two NVs 25 nm apart?" Both sit in one laser spot; they are told apart spectrally (different crystal axes, lines about 30 MHz apart) and read out together. Field gradients and sub-diffraction optics are proposed routes to more. |
| 9 | Slow readout | Data qubits wait during millisecond readout; scaling stops near 100 ms for a 1 s memory; the XZZX code buys about 10× more readout time, because nuclear memories mostly dephase while idle. | "Why is XZZX better here?" It spreads idle dephasing across both logical sectors. Gate bias helps less: with two-level qubits only CZ keeps the bias, and CNOT keeps at most η ≈ 5 [etxezarreta2025]. At bias 100 the native XZZX threshold is 1.81 % against 0.86 % for the standard code; 3.25 % is an upper bound that needs bias-preserving gates. |
| 10 | How big, how slow | Small die, years of runtime: an RSA-2048 job takes about 2.1 years at 1 ms readout in the calibrated model (it reproduces Gidney and Ekerå's 8 hours at their parameters [gidney2021]), and at most 6.0 years if every Toffoli gate is a serial step. Readout speed and link fidelity are the levers, not area or power (machine numbers in chapter E10). | "So is this useless?" For factoring, yes today; for sensing, networking, and embedded quantum memory, room temperature matters more than speed. |
| 11 | Experiments | Anyone can start with a US$150 optically detected magnetic resonance (ODMR) kit; firmware and fitter are in the repository. | "Have you built it?" State honestly what you have and have not built yet. |
| 12 | Takeaways | Four sentences: diamond power wins when hot, not at room temperature with bulk doping; the two-qubit gap is a known discrepancy and addressing is spectral; small but slow; all open and regenerable. | Close by inviting collaboration on the calibration experiments. |

**Tips.** Practice slide 5 and slide 8; they are the two "honest science" moments that tend to impress. If time is short, skip slides 6 and 7. Keep the poster's QR code visible; it opens the interactive site.
